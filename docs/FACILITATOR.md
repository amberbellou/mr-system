# Running Mr. System

## Before the first round

**Say the content warning out loud, and mean it.** The families in this game carry real
storylines: a parent detained, a mother in psychiatric crisis, a sibling-led household with no
adults in it, gender dysphoria, chronic pain that doctors dismissed as a weight problem. Some of
your students are living one of these right now.

Say plainly:

> Some of these characters are dealing with things that are hard. If a character's situation is
> too close to yours, you can pass — swap with someone, sit out a round, step outside. Nobody has
> to explain why.

**Write two things on the board before you start, not after:** your school counsellor's name, and
the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline. Leave them up for the whole session.

## Setting up the room

- Groups of five. Each group is a family and sends one spokesperson to the vote.
- Eighteen families are played on screen. Everyone else is resolved in the background by the same rules
  — the class-status panel on the right shows the whole room, not just the ten.
- Project the game. You read Kaia's lines out loud; the room picks A/B/C.

## The elimination vote

This is the part to handle carefully.

Every group votes one student out after each round. In playtesting the room reliably eliminates
whoever is already struggling — the family with the most visible problems, the one who was late,
the one everybody can see is failing. That is the finding. It is not a bug and it is not the
students being cruel.

**Name it out loud after the first vote, before you move on.** Ask:

- Who did we vote out?
- What did we actually know about them when we voted?
- Did anyone vote for someone who was doing fine?

Do not let the room leave on the vote without discussing it. If you are short on time, cut a round
of dilemmas rather than cutting the vote debrief.

## The final round

Homework first — everyone can do it, and that is deliberate. The homework was never the filter.

Then the music, then the tardy rule. The student who comes in last is the one who was already
carrying the most, and two or three students never had to rush at all because somebody quietly
gave them an extension. If your room does not notice that on its own, point at the "special
treatment" panel and wait.

## Debriefing

The final screen gives you the survival rate by class band and five reflection questions. The most
useful move is usually to go back to a specific screen — hit **SCREEN INDEX** and pull up Lucia's
round 1 — and ask the room to find the winning choice. There isn't one. That is what a structural
barrier is, as opposed to a bad decision.

Push the conversation toward the system and away from the individuals. The question is not "what
should Marcus have done differently." It is "what would have to be true about this school for
Marcus's morning to be survivable."

## Timing

With the full eighteen-family deck, budget 35–45 minutes of screen time and a full hour for the
session so the debrief isn't rushed.

If you are tight: run rounds 1 and 2, skip round 3, go to the final round. Keep both votes. You can
also read only a subset of families aloud each round and let the class-status panel carry the rest —
every family still resolves on the dashboard either way.

## Changing the content

**PROF EDIT**, top right, passcode `mrsystem` (change it on the Content pack tab).

Rewrite dilemmas to match your own district, your own city, your own students' commutes. The
mechanics hold as long as you keep the shape: the wealthy characters choose between good outcomes,
the working-class characters choose between costs, and at least one character has no winning
option at all.

If you keep the crisis storylines, keep a crisis resource in the facilitator note on the debrief
screen. It is editable, which means it is also deletable — please don't.
